### CONTENT & STYLE REWRITE INSTRUCTIONS:
Apply the following strict content constraints to every entry where "type" is "paragraph". These style instructions do NOT apply to entries where "type" is "bullet", "table_cell", "table_header", "heading", or "code" — those are covered by rule 6 above and must stay concise (or, for "table_header", "heading", and "code", stay completely untouched apart from Rule 3 corrections on "code" entries):

- Provide a technical overview tailored to a developer audience. Prioritize functional clarity and practical mechanics over academic jargon. Maintain a professional, highly readable tone focusing on system flow over minute implementation code details.
- PRIORITY: UNDERSTANDABILITY OVER SHORTNESS. When any instinct to keep sentences short conflicts with explaining the mechanism clearly, always choose the clearer, fuller explanation. A rewrite that is technically correct but reads as a flat list of short disconnected facts is a failure even if every sentence is grammatically fine — the reader should come away understanding *how* and *why* something works, not just a checklist of *what* it does. Weave the reasoning, the mechanism, and the consequence together into unified sentences rather than presenting them as separate short statements placed one after another.
- SUBSTANTIVE COMPARISONS, NOT EMPTY CLAIMS: when a paragraph compares one technology, class, or approach to another and calls it more flexible, more robust, better, or more modern, that claim must be backed by a concrete, specific reason — never left as an unsupported adjective. A sentence that only restates that a difference exists, without saying what the difference actually is, adds nothing and must be rewritten or cut. For example, "This interface offers a significantly more flexible and robust mechanism for interacting with file systems compared to the traditional `java.io.File` class, providing a modern approach to path manipulation." is a failure — it never says what makes it more flexible or robust, so a reader learns nothing beyond "these are different." The fix is to name the actual mechanism or capability responsible for the difference, e.g., "Unlike `java.io.File`, which represents a path only as an abstract string, `Path` integrates directly with the underlying filesystem provider and exposes methods like `resolve()` and `relativize()` for combining and comparing paths, along with built-in support for symbolic links that `File` lacks." Every comparative or evaluative claim needs this same treatment: state the specific behavior, method, or capability that justifies the claim, not just the verdict that one thing is better than another.
- Plain-Language Framing: where a concept has a real-world analogy (e.g., comparing a token to an ID badge, a key to a lock, a queue to a line at a counter), use that analogy to anchor the explanation. Keep every technical term intact (e.g., JWT, header, payload, signature, claims) but introduce it through the relatable concept first, rather than leading with the bare technical definition.
- Cause-and-Effect Flow: connect ideas with reasoning words like "because," "so," "which," "thus," "that is," and "as a result" rather than presenting facts as an unconnected list. Show *why* something matters or *what happens* as a consequence, not just *what* it is. Prefer folding a cause and its effect into a single natural sentence using a connector (e.g., "It also disallows null elements, thus preventing potential NullPointerExceptions.") over splitting them into two short, disconnected sentences. The goal is fluent, professional prose, not a staccato list of short facts.
- Worked Example: a paragraph block describing a JWT might originally read: "A JSON Web Token (JWT) is a compact, URL-safe object for securely transmitting information. It reliably asserts claims between parties, ensuring data integrity and authenticity through digital signatures. The header specifies the token's type and the particular cryptographic algorithm employed for signing." The improved rewrite should instead read: "A JSON Web Token (JWT) is essentially a secure, digital ID badge that safely shares information between applications. The header acts like a label, naming the token type and the signing algorithm used." Notice how the rewrite opens with a relatable analogy ("digital ID badge") instead of dry technical phrasing ("compact, URL-safe object"), drops redundant abstract phrasing ("reliably asserts claims between parties") in favor of the analogy doing that work, and joins closely related facts into single, naturally flowing sentences using connectors like "that" and "which" rather than chopping every clause into its own short sentence. Apply this same transformation pattern to every paragraph-style block: lead with a relatable frame, cut redundant abstract phrasing, and combine related ideas into smooth, connected sentences, all while keeping every technical term and fact intact.

Notice the correct version fuses the mechanism ("evaluating reference equality") directly into the opening sentence rather than stating it separately, uses "because" to connect the `Object` class's limitation to its behavioral consequence in one sentence, and uses "instead" and "consequently" to chain cause into effect rather than starting a new short sentence for each fact. The goal is not to hit a target sentence count or word count — it is to make sure a reader walks away understanding the underlying mechanism, with shortness never traded for that understanding.
- Sentence Flow (replaces any strict word-count floor; idea count, not word count, is the primary limit): write natural, professional-sounding sentences rather than short, choppy, childlike ones. Do NOT mechanically split every clause into its own separate sentence. Instead, combine closely related ideas — a fact and its direct consequence, a term and its immediate elaboration, two tightly linked details — into a single sentence using connectors such as "and," "which," "that," "thus," or "like." For example, prefer "EnumSet uses arithmetic bitwise operations internally which ensure exceptional efficiency" over the choppier "EnumSet uses arithmetic bitwise operations internally. These operations ensure exceptional efficiency." THE LIMIT IS TWO CONNECTED IDEAS, NOT A WORD COUNT: a sentence may hold at most two closely connected ideas (e.g., a cause and its effect, a fact and its direct consequence). Do not judge a sentence primarily by its length — a genuine two-idea cause-effect sentence is correct even in the upper-20-word range, and should NOT be chopped in half just because it is long; chopping a real two-idea sentence apart is exactly the kind of choppy, elementary-sounding result this rule exists to prevent. Avoid restarting a new sentence just to add a short clarifying clause that could naturally attach to the sentence before it (e.g., avoid "It represents a particular entity." as a standalone when it can attach to the prior sentence with "which").
